Definitions
Adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM)
A loan whose interest rate is fixed for an initial period and then changes at set intervals.
Initial (fixed) period
The months before the first rate change. Your rate and payment stay the same during this time.
Reset
A date when the rate is recalculated. The payment is then recalculated so the loan still ends on schedule.
Index
A published benchmark rate that the lender follows, such as SOFR. When the index moves, your rate moves with it.
Margin
A fixed number of percentage points the lender adds to the index. Your fully indexed rate is the index plus the margin. This calculator asks for the change you expect instead, so it does not use an index or margin.
Initial adjustment cap
The most the rate can change at the first reset.
Periodic (subsequent) adjustment cap
The most the rate can change at each reset after the first.
Lifetime cap
The most the rate can rise above the starting rate over the whole loan.
Floor
The lowest rate the loan can ever have, even if the index falls further.
Interest-only period
A stretch at the start when the required payment covers only interest, so the balance does not fall (apart from any extra you pay). The payment rises when it ends.
Break-even point
How long after a refinance it takes for the money saved on payments to cover the closing costs.
Payment shock
A large jump in the monthly payment after a reset. This calculator flags it when the worst-case payment is 25% or more above the first payment.
How adjustable-rate mortgages work

An ARM usually starts with a rate below a comparable fixed-rate loan. A name such as 5/1 means the rate is fixed for 5 years and then adjusts every year. A 2/1/5 cap structure means the rate can rise at most 2 points at the first reset, 1 point at each later reset, and 5 points over the starting rate in total.

After the initial period, the rate is the index plus the margin, limited by the caps and the floor. If the index rises, the rate and payment rise, up to the caps. If the index falls, they fall, down to the floor.

Who an ARM may suit

  • People who expect to sell or refinance before the first reset.
  • People whose income is likely to grow and who could afford the worst-case payment.
  • People who want the lower starting payment and are comfortable with the risk that it rises.

Who should be careful

  • People who plan to stay in the home a long time on a tight budget.
  • Anyone who could not afford the payment at the lifetime cap rate. Check the worst case above.

For a fuller explanation, see the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's guide to loan options (opens in a new tab).

Methodology and assumptions
  • The loan is paid monthly. Interest each month is the balance times the annual rate divided by 12.
  • The payment is the standard amortizing payment on the remaining balance over the remaining months. It is recalculated at each rate reset, so the loan still ends at the end of the term.
  • The first reset comes after the initial period. Later resets come at the interval you set. With index + margin, the rate at each reset targets the index plus the margin, where the index starts at the value you enter and moves by your assumed change at every reset. With a fixed change, the target is the previous rate plus that change. Either way the move is limited by the cap on the first adjustment or on each later one, then held between the lowest possible rate and the starting rate plus the lifetime cap. The index values offered when you pick an index are illustrative; enter the current published figure.
  • Best, expected and worst case: the expected case uses your assumed change. The best case lowers the rate by the full cap on a single adjustment at every reset, down to the lowest possible rate. The worst case raises it by the full cap at every reset, up to the starting rate plus the lifetime cap. A payment rise of 25% or more over the first payment in the worst case is flagged as payment shock.
  • The comparison is the same loan held at the starting rate for the whole term.
  • Optional extras: property tax, insurance and HOA dues are held flat as entered. PMI is the entered percentage of the original loan per year, charged while the balance is above 80% of the home price. The down payment is the home price minus the loan amount, and cash needed at closing is the down payment plus closing costs.
  • Extra payment: the amount you enter is paid on top of the required payment every month and goes entirely to principal. The required payment is not lowered, so the loan ends early. One-time lump sums are not modelled.
  • Interest only: for the months you enter, the required payment is the month's interest. Afterwards it is recalculated, as at a reset, to repay the balance over the months left in the term, so it jumps. Payment-option loans that let the balance grow (negative amortization) are not modelled.
  • Refinance: after the number of payments you enter, the remaining balance is replaced by a new fixed-rate loan with the rate and term you give. Closing costs are paid in cash at that point. Both paths are totalled from the refinance month until each is paid off, and the break-even point is the first month the refinance's cumulative cost, costs included, is no more than keeping the loan. Any extra payment continues on both.
  • Not modelled: points, one-time lump-sum payments, rising taxes or insurance, and rates that move by different amounts at different resets.

Not financial advice. This calculator gives estimates based on the inputs and simplifications described above. Real loans differ; check your loan's terms and talk to a qualified professional before borrowing.
