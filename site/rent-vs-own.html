Rent vs own

Compare buying a home with renting a similar one and investing the difference. Enter the home price and today's rent; the calculator shows which leaves you with more money after the years you plan to stay.

Starting point:

Methodology and assumptions
  • Owning: you pay the down payment and closing costs, then the monthly mortgage payment (a standard fixed-rate loan) plus property tax, insurance and maintenance, each a percentage of the home's value that year (property tax is a percentage of the assessed value, which follows the home's value unless you enter a slower assessed growth rate; see below), plus any HOA dues and extra utilities (beyond what you would pay as a renter) that you enter in dollars per month and that rise once a year by their own growth rates. The home's value grows at the rate you enter. When you sell, selling costs are deducted, the loan is repaid and tax is paid on the gain. The gain is the sale price less selling costs less the price and closing costs you paid; the primary-residence exclusion you enter ($250,000 single, $500,000 married filing jointly) comes off first, and only when you stay at least two years (the ownership-and-use test). The rest is taxed at the same capital gains rate as the renter's investments. Your net worth is what is left.
  • Renting: you pay rent (rising once a year by the rent growth) plus renter's insurance, and invest the down payment and closing costs you did not spend. Each month, whatever owning costs beyond renting is also invested; if renting costs more, the shortfall is taken from the investments. Investments grow at the return you enter, less a yearly tax drag (a percentage of the balance, never more than that month's gain). When you sell, capital gains tax is charged on the balance above the money you put in. Your net worth is the investment balance after that tax. This is the equal-cash view, and the headline follows it.
  • The page also shows a pure net worth view: the renter keeps only the invested down payment and closing costs, taxed the same way, and the monthly difference between owning and renting is not credited to either side. The two views can name different winners; the page says so when they do.
  • Inflation: future dollars are the amounts you would actually see in each year. Today's dollars divide each year's net worth, for both paths, by (1 + inflation) for every year that has passed, so they are comparable to money now. The inflation input only changes the today's-dollars view; the model's own growth rates are not tied to it. At 0% inflation the two views agree.
  • Cash needed to buy is the down payment plus closing costs, moving costs and a reserve of months of owning costs you choose (default none). If you enter cash saved and it is less than that total, the result says you are short instead of naming a winner, and estimates the months to close the gap by saving what owning would cost each month beyond rent (interest earned ignored).
  • The follow-through quotes the year 1 monthly difference between owning and renting (the amount a renter must invest each month, or the extra an owner pays). It is not the total difference spread over the stay. The checklist covers things the calculator does not model: how long you will stay, flexibility, maintenance and job or location risk.
  • The cost breakdown splits the gap at the end of your stay into four parts that add up to it: the down payment and closing costs; the recurring cost of owning beyond what renting would cost; the opportunity cost, which is the investment return that money would have earned if you had rented instead; and the money back when you sell, which is subtracted. Mortgage payments are counted in full, and the principal comes back through the loan payoff.
  • Rent, then buy: for the years you enter, this path is the equal-cash renter. Then it sells the investments (paying the capital gains tax), buys the same home at its appreciated price, pays closing costs on that price and puts what is left down, with a new loan on the balance at the rate you enter (blank: the mortgage rate above) over the same term. If the savings cover more than the price, the rest stays invested. After the purchase the monthly budget is still what buying now would cost, so a cheaper or dearer payment is invested or drawn from the investments just as the renter's difference is. It counts the home-sale tax from the purchase date (the exclusion needs two years in the home) and a major repair counts from the purchase. It does not check whether you would qualify for the loan. With 0 years it equals buying now; with the whole stay it equals renting.
  • The break-even price and rent are solved by searching for the home price (or monthly rent) at which the net worth gap at the end of your stay is $0, with every other input as you entered it. If several values qualify it shows the one nearest your entry; if none does within the search range it says so.
  • The price-to-rent line divides the home price by a year of rent and shows rent as a share of price. The rule of thumb is that renting wins when yearly rent is below a certain share of the price; here that share is the break-even rent from the search above, so it moves with your mortgage rate, stay and other inputs rather than being a fixed 5% or 7%. It is a quick screen that ignores everything else the model weighs, such as how fast rent and the home’s value grow.
  • When the gap at the end of your stay is under $5,000, or under 5% of the larger side’s net worth, the headline says “Too close to call” instead of naming a winner, because a small change in any growth rate would reverse it. The gap is still shown, and the table of what flips the verdict shows how far each assumption would have to move.
  • Break-even is the first year after which owning stays ahead through the end of your stay.
  • The chart runs to the break-even year or 30 years, whichever comes first, and marks your stay separately, so you can see where the lines cross even when that is after you plan to leave. The table lists the years of your stay.
  • The cases table reruns the comparison with the three growth rates moved together: the case good for owning raises home growth by 2 points and rent growth by 1 point and lowers the investment return by 1 point; the case bad for owning does the opposite. The expected case uses your inputs exactly.
  • Mortgage insurance (PMI) is a percentage of the original loan per year, charged each month while the loan balance is above 80% of the home’s value (so it ends as you pay down and the home appreciates) and counted in the monthly cost of owning.
  • Loan type: Conventional charges the PMI percentage described above. FHA charges an upfront mortgage insurance premium of 1.75% of the base loan, either added to the loan or paid in cash at closing (your choice), plus an annual premium of 0.55% of the base loan (0.50% when the loan is 90% or less of the price) paid monthly; it does not stop at 80% of the home’s value, and lasts the life of the loan when the original loan is above 90% of the price and 11 years otherwise (HUD FHA Single Family Housing Policy Handbook 4000.1). VA charges a one-time funding fee, added to the loan or paid in cash, and no monthly insurance: 2.15% of the loan for first use (3.3% for subsequent use) with less than 5% down, 1.5% with 5% to under 10% down and 1.25% with 10% or more down (VA funding fee table). A cash fee is part of the cash needed to buy and of the money the renter would have invested instead. The PMI percentage input applies to conventional loans only. The presets do not change the loan type.
  • An optional one-time major repair (a roof, say) is paid by the owner in the first month of the year you choose, on top of the maintenance percentage; it reduces the Own net worth by its cost plus the investment return that money would have earned, and does not affect renting.
  • The monthly breakdown shows the first month of the first and last year of your stay; mortgage principal is shown separately because it builds your equity, so the true cost of owning leaves it out.
  • Mortgage type: Fixed rate (the default) uses one rate for the whole loan. Adjustable rate (ARM) uses the same reset rules as the variable-rate mortgage calculator: the starting rate holds for the months you enter, then at each reset the rate targets the index plus the margin (the index moving by your assumed change at every reset), limited by the cap on the first adjustment, the cap on each later one, the lifetime cap above the starting rate and the lowest possible rate. The payment is recalculated at each reset to repay the balance over the months left. With the ARM chosen, the cases table also shows the fixed-rate loan beside it.
  • Assessment caps: some places limit how fast the taxable value of a home can rise, whatever the market does. California's Proposition 13 (Article XIIIA of the state constitution) limits the yearly rise in assessed value to 2% until the home is sold; Florida's Save Our Homes cap is 3% for homesteads. Enter the cap as the assessed value growth (or use the one-click buttons) and each year's property tax is the tax rate times the assessed value. Left blank, the assessed value follows the home's value. The taxed value is never more than the home's market value, so a cap can lower the tax but never raise it. The model keeps the cap in force over the whole stay.
  • The default mortgage rate is the latest Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey 30-year fixed average, kept in a data file that is refreshed on a regular schedule; the survey week and the date the defaults were last refreshed are shown under the inputs. If the file cannot be read, the calculator falls back to a built-in 6.5%. Any rate you enter, or that arrives in a shared link, replaces the default.
  • Not modelled: income tax effects of owning (the mortgage interest and property tax deductions), and the ARM options of interest-only periods and extra payments.
  • Inflation only changes the today's-dollars view.
  • The investment return is a steady assumption, not a forecast.
  • The sensitivity table moves each of five inputs on its own (mortgage rate 1 point, the growth and return rates 2 points, stay length 3 years) and shows the break-even year and the end-of-stay gap at each; the break-even year does not depend on your stay, so stay length moves only the gap.
  • The investment return presets are long-run planning averages in nominal terms, not forecasts: Aggressive 10% (100% stocks, in line with the S&P 500’s long-run average of about 10% nominal, or 7% after inflation), Moderate 8% (60% stocks, 40% bonds), Conservative 6% (20% stocks, 80% bonds) and Savings 4% (a savings account). Picking one also suggests an annual tax drag (0.7% to 1%, higher for the more conservative mixes whose return arrives as taxable interest). Both fields stay editable, and the defaults (5% and 0.7%) apply until you pick a preset.

Not financial advice. This calculator gives estimates based on the inputs and simplifications described above. Real costs and returns differ; talk to a qualified professional before buying a home.
