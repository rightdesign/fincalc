Why compounding is so powerful

Methodology and assumptions
  • By default contributions are made at the end of each month, so the first one starts earning in month two. Choose “Beginning of month” and each contribution is added before that month's interest, so it earns one extra month of growth; the formula above then gains a factor of (1 + r/12) on the contribution term.
  • Monthly compounding: each month the balance earns the annual return divided by 12, and then your contribution is added. The future value is FV = S × (1 + r/12)n + C × ((1 + r/12)n − 1) / (r/12), where S is the starting balance, C the monthly contribution, r the annual return and n the number of months. At a 0% return it is S + C × n.
  • Annual compounding: the balance at the start of each year earns the full annual return once, at year end. Contributions made during the year earn simple interest for the months they were invested, and join the balance at year end.
  • Semiannual and quarterly compounding: the same rule with shorter periods. The balance at the start of each period earns the annual return divided by 2 (or 4) once, at period end, and contributions made during the period earn simple interest for the months left in it.
  • Daily and continuous compounding: the balance grows by the equivalent of a month of daily compounding, (1 + r/365)365/12, each month (or er/12 for continuous), and contributions are added at the end or beginning of the month as above. Monthly contributions are the only deposits modeled, so these differ from monthly compounding only by the growth within each month.
  • Two phases: when Contribute for is shorter than Years, the plan runs in two phases. For the first N years (the contribution months) the formula above applies with n = 12 × N, giving the balance B. For the remaining M years there are no contributions, so the balance only compounds: FV = B × (1 + r/12)12M. In the formula, the contribution term runs for the contribution months only; the starting balance compounds for all the months.
  • Total contributed is the starting balance plus every monthly contribution. If you set Contribute for to fewer years than Years, the contributions stop after that many years and the balance keeps compounding, with no new money, until the end. Interest earned is the future value minus total contributed.
  • Cost of waiting: the comparison reruns your plan with ten fewer years (same starting balance, contribution, return and compounding) and ends on the same date, so the difference is what ten extra years of saving adds. It appears when Years is more than 10. If Contribute for is shorter than the horizon, both sides stop contributing after the same number of years (or when their horizon ends, if sooner). In today's dollars, both end balances are divided by (1 + inflation)years and the difference is recomputed from them; contributions stay as paid.
  • The return is a constant yearly rate. Real returns vary, and fees are not included.
  • Tax on interest: at 0% (the default) nothing is taxed. Set a marginal tax rate t and each period's interest (each month's with monthly, daily or continuous compounding; each period's with annual, semiannual or quarterly) is multiplied by (1 − t) before it joins the balance, so the tax is paid out of the interest as it is earned and only the after-tax part compounds. In every case the rate that compounds is r × (1 − t): monthly growth is r/12 × (1 − t), and continuous compounding grows at er(1 − t). The balances, interest and every comparison on the page are then after tax. It models a taxable account where the interest or dividends are taxed each year; it does not model capital-gains tax on a sale, tax-deferred accounts, or a rate that changes over time.
  • Return variance: optional, 0 by default. When set, the plan is run two more times at the return minus and plus the variance (the lower rate never goes below 0%), each still a constant yearly rate. The chart shades the area between those two balances and the results state the resulting range. It is a what-if range, not a statistical confidence interval: real returns can land outside it.
  • Today's dollars: when selected, each year's balance is divided by (1 + inflation)year, so it shows what that balance could buy at today's prices. Total contributed stays in the dollars you paid in, and the interest line becomes the restated balance minus that (it can be negative if inflation outruns the return). The year-by-year interest columns are unchanged. Switching views never changes the future-dollar figures.
